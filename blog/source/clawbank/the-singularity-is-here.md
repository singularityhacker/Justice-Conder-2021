We've crossed the event horizon, and 90% of the economy can't see it. That figure comes straight from the latest numbers reported by The Economist:

> Nine in ten executives report no impact from AI on their firm's productivity over the past three years.

The divergence of experience couldn't be more stark. On X, there are miracles every day such that some are calling it the age of magic. Solo builders ship in a weekend what would've taken a funded team a year. Inside the big corporate offices, it's business as usual. The new miraculous capabilities are actively hidden and intentionally sabotaged from the inside.

At a time when most crypto people are saying the era of life-changing opportunities has ended, I suspect this is that new opportunity. A secret this size usually costs something to learn. This one requires one thing: agency.

## Enterprise AI Adoption Numbers

Big tech dropped $450 billion on AI infrastructure last year, will spend $900 billion this year, and has $1.4 trillion queued for 2027. To make that math work, the industry needs roughly $2.5 trillion a year in AI revenue, more than all of tech's combined revenue today.

And the enterprises who are supposed to supply that revenue? Here are their usage levels:

- The average American executive uses AI for 1.7 hours a week. Enough time to make a decent PowerPoint, per the Bank of England's own researchers.
- About half of German firms using AI spend 5% of working hours or less on it (Bundesbank).
- Only a tenth of euro-area companies using AI report doing so "intensively" (ECB).
- Close to half of British businesses using AI don't pay a dime for it.
- The median firm's monthly AI spend per worker, per Ramp's data: $10.66.

Corporations effectively bought chatbot subscriptions and changed nothing else. They bolted a jet engine onto a horse cart and are wondering why they're not flying.

## Corporate Employee AI Sabotage

For decades, there were two career paths. Path one: get a corporate job, do the bare minimum, ride the gravy train to retirement. Path two: go entrepreneur, walk through the gauntlet, suffer, and maybe capture extreme upside. Most people rationally picked path one. It paid fine, and the downside was capped.

AI amplifies both paths, but in opposite directions.

For the high-agency entrepreneur, these tools are a force multiplier with no ceiling. One person can now do market research, write production code, draft contracts, run support, and manage a treasury before lunch.

For the corporate lifer, the same tools are a threat. If an agent can do 60% of your job, demonstrating that fact is career suicide. So the rational move is quiet sabotage: don't use the tools, or use them badly. Burn expensive inference on tasks that should be one-time code generation. Run the same bloated prompt through a frontier model 400 times a day when an agent could've written a script once and dropped the inference cost to zero. Make AI look expensive and useless, because your fiefdom depends on it looking expensive and useless.

I don't think most people are doing this consciously. But incentives are incentives. The Economist's data reads exactly like what you'd expect from an immune system: adoption flat, zero process change, and 90% of executives shrugging their shoulders.

Enterprises have no idea how to manage this, because the org chart itself is the thing being threatened. You can't ask middle management to automate middle management.

## The Single Founder Opportunity

My prediction: Big companies are going to lose their head start. Gradually, then all at once.

Their advantages were always coordination advantages: capital, headcount, distribution, the ability to throw 5,000 people at a problem. AI collapses the value of headcount and hands coordination to anyone with an API key. What's left is an expensive building full of people whose incentive is to slow the technology down.

Meanwhile, the small teams have no immune system to fight. A 2-person company has nobody whose job depends on AI failing. Every hour of leverage goes straight to output. The productivity gains that are invisible in the enterprise data are compounding daily on the outside.

This is why the single most important product category right now is the one-person company, and its logical endpoint, the zero-employee company. The idea is simple: give a single motivated founder what previously took company-sized resources. Formation, banking, contracts, treasury, trading, compliance, all bundled and operable by agents instead of departments.

The corporate world will read the Economist piece and conclude AI is overhyped. The builders will read it and see the largest arbitrage of their lifetime: a superpower suite that incumbents are institutionally incapable of using.
